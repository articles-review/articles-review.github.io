Narwhal and Tusk: Moving the Bottleneck Out of BFT Consensus

October 13, 2026
Tags: consensus byzantine-fault-tolerance distributed-systems blockchain mempool

For most of the last decade, work on fast Byzantine fault-tolerant (BFT) consensus has focused on one number: message complexity, meaning how many protocol messages the validators exchange per decision. HotStuff (Yin et al., 2019) brought that number down to linear in the partially synchronous setting by routing all votes through a leader. The paper by Danezis, Kokoris-Kogias, Sonnino and Spiegelman, published at EuroSys 2022, argues that this target misses where the cost actually sits in a real ledger. The expensive part is not agreeing on an order. It is moving the transactions themselves to every validator, reliably.

The authors build two systems around that claim. Narwhal is a mempool, the layer that collects and spreads pending transactions, redesigned as a round-based directed acyclic graph (DAG) of certified blocks. Tusk is an asynchronous consensus protocol that orders that DAG without sending a single extra message. On a wide-area network, the paper reports that HotStuff running over Narwhal reaches over 130,000 transactions per second at under 2 seconds of latency, against 1,800 tx/s for a conventional HotStuff deployment, and that adding worker machines per validator pushes throughput to about 600,000 tx/s with no increase in latency.

Why message complexity is the wrong thing to minimize

The paper opens with two observations about leader-based protocols. First, a protocol that minimizes total message count by funneling everything through a leader concentrates load on that leader, which becomes the bottleneck. Second, message complexity counts metadata: votes, signatures and hashes on the order of 100 bytes. Blocks of transactions are on the order of 10 MB. For committees up to roughly 50 nodes, the asymptotic difference between linear and quadratic metadata is dwarfed by the cost of shipping bulk data.

There is also a structural point. In ledgers like Bitcoin or LibraBFT (now DiemBFT), clients send transactions to a validator, a gossip mempool spreads them around, and then the leader re-sends a subset of them inside a consensus proposal. Transactions cross the network twice, and the second crossing sits on the critical path of consensus.

The paper states its hypothesis directly: a better mempool, one that reliably distributes transactions, is the key enabler of a high-performance ledger. It should be separated from consensus altogether, leaving consensus only the job of ordering small fixed-size references. If this is right, system throughput should be largely unaffected by consensus throughput, and different consensus protocols layered on top should differ mainly in latency.

Earlier systems such as Prism (Bagaria et al., 2019) and work on PBFT-style batching already separated large payloads from metadata. The authors’ criticism is that they did so without guaranteeing reliability: they work well with no faults and degrade badly at the first network hiccup. To test this, the authors build Batched-HS, a HotStuff variant where validators broadcast batches and the leader proposes only their hashes. It serves as the representative of that prior approach throughout the evaluation.

The mempool as a certified key-value store

Narwhal is specified as an abstraction before it is described as a protocol. The model is a message-passing system of nnn parties, a computationally bounded adversary that controls the network and can corrupt up to f<n/3f < n/3f<n/3 parties, and asynchronous eventually reliable links: no bound on delay, and a finite but unknown number of lost messages.

A block bbb contains a list of transactions and a list of references to earlier blocks. Its cryptographic digest ddd serves as its identifier. A reference from bbb to b′b'b′ encodes a happened-before relation, written b′→bb' \to bb′→b, and by convention all referenced blocks happen before all transactions in the block.

The abstraction offers four operations. write(d,b)\mathrm{write}(d, b)write(d,b) stores a block and returns c(d)c(d)c(d), an unforgeable certificate of availability; the write succeeds when the certificate forms. valid(d,c(d))\mathrm{valid}(d, c(d))valid(d,c(d)) checks a certificate. read(d)\mathrm{read}(d)read(d) returns the block. read_causal(d)\mathrm{read\_causal}(d)read_causal(d) returns the set BBB of all blocks with a transitive happened-before path to the block with digest ddd.

Narwhal guarantees five properties:

  • Integrity: for any certified digest ddd, any two honest reads that return a value return the same value.
  • Block-Availability: if an honest party invokes read(d)\mathrm{read}(d)read(d) after write(d,b)\mathrm{write}(d,b)write(d,b) has succeeded, the read eventually returns bbb.
  • Containment: if BBB is returned by read_causal(d)\mathrm{read\_causal}(d)read_causal(d), then for every b′∈Bb' \in Bb′∈B, the set B′B'B′ returned by read_causal(d′)\mathrm{read\_causal}(d')read_causal(d′) satisfies B′⊆BB' \subseteq BB′⊆B.
  • 2/3-Causality: read_causal(d)\mathrm{read\_causal}(d)read_causal(d) returns at least 2/3 of the blocks written successfully before write(d,b)\mathrm{write}(d,b)write(d,b) was invoked.
  • 1/2-Chain Quality: at least half of the blocks in a returned causal set were written by honest parties.

Each property has a job. Integrity and Block-Availability let consensus order only short certificates, since the content behind them is guaranteed to exist and be unique. Causality and Containment mean that once consensus agrees on one digest, everything it causally depends on can be ordered too, including blocks produced while consensus was stalled. Chain Quality is what gives censorship resistance. The authors also add a non-theoretical goal, scale-out: throughput should grow linearly with the resources each validator commits, without hurting latency.

Five steps from gossip to Narwhal

The paper motivates the design by evolving a plain gossip mempool in five steps.

Step one broadcasts blocks rather than individual transactions and lets the leader propose a block hash. This removes the double transmission, but validators must still download a block before they can vouch for its hash, and that download is on the critical path.

Step two consistently broadcasts each block so that it produces a certificate proving the block will be available. The leader proposes certificates. The remaining problem is that every mempool block needs its own certificate in the proposal, and if consensus loses liveness for a while, the backlog of certificates to commit grows without bound.

Step three adds causality. Mempool blocks include certificates of earlier mempool blocks from all validators, so one certificate stands for a block and its entire causal history. A leader proposing one fixed-size certificate thereby proposes an extension of the sequence covering everything in that history. Consensus delays now cost latency but not average throughput, since blocks keep being produced and are committed later in bulk.

Two issues remain at this stage: a very fast validator could flood others with blocks they must download, and slow honest validators might never get their blocks referenced. Step four imposes rounds. Each block carries a round number and must include a quorum of certificates from the previous round. A validator cannot outrun the honest majority, and every proposal necessarily contains a fraction of honest blocks. The authors claim this makes Narwhal-HotStuff the only partially synchronous quorum-based protocol with censorship resistance in the sense of HoneyBadgerBFT (Miller et al., 2016), without threshold encryption: an adversary gains nothing by killing leaders whose proposals it dislikes, because every proposal includes at least 50% honest content.

Step five splits each validator into one primary and many workers, which handle batches of transactions while the primary handles only hashes. This is the scale-out step.

Rounds, certificates and the block DAG

Each validator keeps a local round rrr, starting at zero. It accumulates client transactions into a transaction list and certificates for round r−1r-1r−1 into a certificate list. Once it holds certificates from 2f+12f+12f+1 distinct validators for round r−1r-1r−1, it advances to round rrr and broadcasts a new block containing its identity, the round number, its pending transactions, the round r−1r-1r−1 certificates, and its signature. Correct validators create one block per round.

Figure 1 is the paper’s picture of three rounds of this process with four validators, so f=1f=1f=1. Each stack in a Blocks column is one validator’s block for that round, and the shaded slot in it is the block’s certificate list. Follow the arrows out of that slot: each points to a certificate of the previous round, and there are 2f+1=32f+1 = 32f+1=3 of them. The short arrow from a certificate points back to the block it certifies. In round rrr only three of the four blocks have a certificate so far, and that is enough for every validator to build its block for round r+1r+1r+1. The lower part of the figure lists what a block and a certificate contain.

Other validators check the block and, if it is valid, store it and reply with a signature over its digest, round and creator. A block is valid if it carries a valid creator signature, is at the checker’s current round, is either a round-0 genesis block or contains at least 2f+12f+12f+1 certificates from round r−1r-1r−1, and is the first block received from that creator for round rrr. A block from a future round carries the 2f+12f+12f+1 certificates that let the checker advance its own round and then sign. When the creator collects 2f+12f+12f+1 signatures, it combines them into a certificate of availability and sends that certificate to everyone, who will reference it in round r+1r+1r+1.

The exchange for one block is two messages out and one back, drawn as a sequence diagram in Figure 2. The block exists as soon as validator A sends it, but nobody may reference it until the certificate comes back around.

The security intuition is quorum intersection. A certificate has 2f+12f+12f+1 signatures, so at least f+1f+1f+1 honest validators checked and stored the block: it can always be fetched. Since honest validators sign only one block per creator per round, two conflicting blocks for the same slot can never both gather 2f+12f+12f+1 signatures, which prevents equivocation. Because each block references certificates from the previous round, induction shows every block in a certified block’s causal history is itself certified and available.

The appendix turns this into proofs. Integrity follows from collision resistance of the hash. Block-Availability follows because at least f+1f+1f+1 honest validators store each certified block, and a read that waits for n−fn-fn−f replies reaches at least one of them. Chain Quality follows because each round in a causal history contains at least 2f+12f+12f+1 blocks, of which at most fff are Byzantine. Causality follows because each round has at most 3f+13f+13f+1 written blocks and a causal history picks up at least 2f+12f+12f+1 of them. Containment follows because uniqueness of certified blocks per creator and round means all honest validators agree on the references inside every certified block, and this applies recursively.

Putting HotStuff on top

Narwhal can be used in two ways: an existing consensus protocol orders Narwhal’s certificates, or the DAG itself is read as a consensus protocol once a random coin is added. Figure 3 shows both, separated by the dashed line. In the first, a partially synchronous protocol such as HotStuff or LibraBFT runs on top. Instead of proposing a block of transactions, the leader proposes one or more Narwhal certificates. When a certificate commits, its full uncommitted causal history is ordered by a deterministic rule. Since Narwhal guarantees that everyone sees the same causal history for a given certificate, every validator derives the same total order.

The paper lists two gains. Without faults, a leader broadcasting transactions saturates its own bandwidth while others sit idle; Narwhal spreads the bulk transfer evenly. During asynchrony or under a Byzantine leader, a partially synchronous protocol makes no progress, and with a naive mempool throughput drops to zero. Narwhal keeps certifying blocks at full speed regardless, and the next successful commit sweeps up everything produced in the meantime. Latency still suffers during those periods, which is the gap Tusk is designed to close.

The paper summarizes the theory in a comparison table, with latency counted in round trips or certificates. In the average case, HotStuff takes 3, Narwhal-HotStuff 4 and Tusk 4.5. With fff crashed leaders, both HotStuff variants have O(n)O(n)O(n) worst-case latency while Tusk stays at 4.5. Under asynchrony, only Tusk has bounded latency, 7. On an unstable network that allows one commit between asynchronous periods, Narwhal-HotStuff and Tusk keep synchrony-level throughput while HotStuff does not; under full asynchrony, only Tusk does.

Garbage collection with fixed memory

The authors present garbage collection as a theoretical contribution, since it has been a roadblock for DAG-based protocols such as Hashgraph (Baird, 2016). A DAG is a local structure that converges across validators eventually but with no time bound, so validators may need to retain all history to help peers catch up and to validate arbitrarily old messages. A footnote records that a garbage collection bug in their own implementation consumed 120 GB of RAM within minutes, against a normal footprint of 700 MB.

Narwhal’s round structure solves the validation side: deciding whether to sign a block requires only knowledge of the current round, and a certified block can be checked with verification keys alone. The remaining danger is that two validators garbage-collect different rounds and so disagree on the causal history of a newly committed block. Narwhal therefore uses the consensus layer to agree on the garbage collection round. Older blocks can be moved off the validator, and older messages ignored.

If an adversary delays a block until it is collected, the honest node that created it re-injects its transactions into a later block. Individual DAG blocks may be censored, but every transaction is eventually included, and by 1/2-Chain Quality enters the consensus output within a constant number of rounds. The upshot is O(n)O(n)O(n) in-memory state per validator, covering the current round. Older certified blocks can be served by a passive store or a content distribution network, since certificates already guarantee their integrity.

Reliable broadcast without perfect links

Textbook reliable broadcast, such as double-echo, assumes perfect point-to-point links that retransmit forever, which requires unbounded application memory. TCP drops all state when a connection breaks, and acknowledgments do not help against Byzantine peers who never send them.

Narwhal relies on its quorums instead. Once a validator moves to round r+1r+1r+1, it stops retransmitting and drops all undelivered messages for earlier rounds. A certificate by itself does not give the totality property of reliable broadcast, since some honest nodes may have the block and others not. But when a validator receives a certified block for round r+1r+1r+1, it can pull every missing block in its causal history from the signers of the corresponding certificates. Since at least f+1f+1f+1 honest validators store each block, the probability of a correct reply grows exponentially with the number of validators asked. Only O(1)O(1)O(1) requests per block are active at a time, O(1)O(1)O(1) on average suffice, and an adversary attacking links can force at most O(n)O(n)O(n), which matches the worst-case lower bound of Dolev and Reischuk (1985). Retransmission storage is bounded by O(n)O(n)O(n).

Primaries, workers and streaming batches

Any mempool is ultimately bounded by what a single validator can receive and store. The paper’s observation is that a validator is a unit of trust, not a single computer. Each validator becomes one primary machine and several worker machines. Figure 4 is the paper’s drawing of one round with four validators, one per row, and three workers each. Every worker produces batches, the three batches of a validator feed the primary block to their right, and the block gets its certificate in the last column. The dotted vertical lines join the corresponding workers of the four validators.

Figure 4 shows the data structures and leaves the messages implicit. Figure 5 draws them for two validators with two workers each. The thing to look at is which links cross the validator boundary: transaction data moves only between workers, and the primaries exchange blocks, signatures and certificates that contain nothing larger than a hash.

A load balancer spreads incoming transactions across workers. Each worker forms batches, sends them to the matching worker at every other validator, and after a quorum acknowledges, hands the batch hash to its primary. The primary runs Narwhal unchanged, except that its blocks contain batch hashes instead of transactions, and it signs another validator’s block only once its own workers have stored the referenced batches. By induction, everything under a certificate remains retrievable. Missing batches are pulled worker to worker at the primary’s instruction, and the pull is retried only for the duration of the triggering block’s round.

Workers stream batches continuously, sized at a few hundred to a few thousand transactions, around 500 KB. Most batches reach other validators before the primary block that references them, which shortens the time to sign and keeps the network busy while validators wait for 2f+12f+12f+1 primary blocks.

The authors estimate when the primary would become the bottleneck. A batch of 1,000 transactions of 512 bytes is 512 KB, while its entry in a primary block is a 32-byte hash plus 8 bytes of worker ID, 40 bytes in total. By their arithmetic, about 12,000 workers would be needed before the primary handles data volumes comparable to a single worker. They report never observing the primary as a bottleneck and leave accumulators like Merkle roots to future work.

Tusk: consensus read off the DAG

Tusk starts from DAG-Rider (Keidar et al., 2021), inherits its safety argument, and modifies it for implementation and lower common-case latency. Validators run Narwhal and additionally place in each block a share of a distributed perfect random coin, built from an adaptively secure threshold signature scheme whose key setup can itself be done under asynchrony. Each validator then interprets its own local DAG to derive the total order. No consensus message is ever sent.

The DAG is divided into waves of three consecutive rounds. In the first round, validators propose by creating blocks. In the second, they vote by referencing first-round blocks. In the third, they reveal coin shares that retroactively elect one first-round block as the wave’s leader. As an optimization, the third round of one wave doubles as the first round of the next. A validator commits the elected leader if, in its local view, at least f+1f+1f+1 second-round blocks reference it.

Different validators may see different local DAGs, so one may commit a leader that another skips. To reconcile, after committing the leader of wave www, a validator walks back to the last wave w′w'w′ where it committed. For each wave in between, from latest to earliest, if there is a path from the current candidate to that wave’s leader, the earlier leader is ordered first and becomes the new candidate. Each leader’s causal history is then ordered by a fixed deterministic rule.

Why every honest validator commits the same leaders

Safety rests on two lemmas.

Lemma 1: if an honest validator commits leader block bbb in wave iii, then any leader block b′b'b′ committed by any honest validator vvv in a later wave has a path to bbb in vvv’s local DAG.

The reason is quorum intersection. Committing bbb requires f+1f+1f+1 second-round blocks linking to it. Every block in the next round links to 2f+12f+12f+1 blocks of the round before, and any set of 2f+12f+12f+1 blocks out of at most 3f+13f+13f+1 must intersect any set of f+1f+1f+1. So every block in the first round of wave i+1i+1i+1 has a path to bbb, and by induction so does every block in every later round.

Lemma 2: any two honest validators commit the same sequence of block leaders.

The appendix derives it from Lemma A.6: if an honest validator commits bbb before b′b'b′, no honest validator commits b′b'b′ without first committing bbb. Otherwise, one validator’s order implies there is no path between the two leaders, contradicting Lemma 1. Combined with Narwhal’s Containment property, which ensures every validator sees the same causal history behind each leader, all honest validators agree on the total order of the DAG’s blocks.

What the evaluation shows: the mempool sets throughput, consensus sets latency

The evaluation has four claims to support: Narwhal beats both a gossip mempool and plain batching, adding workers raises throughput linearly, Tusk keeps its throughput as the committee grows, and all of this survives faults. Sections 7.1 to 7.3 of the paper answer with one experiment each.

Implementation and testbed

The validator is written in Rust using Tokio for asynchronous networking, ed25519-dalek for signatures and RocksDB for persistence, in about 4,000 lines plus 2,000 lines of tests. Messages are held in memory and resent over persistent TCP connections that are re-established on failure, until the protocol logic decides they are no longer needed, which bounds memory spent on unreachable peers. For comparison the authors implement HotStuff themselves, adding persistent storage and a pacemaker following the LibraBFT specification, in two versions: Baseline-HS with a gossip mempool and Batched-HS as described earlier.

Experiments run on AWS m5.8xlarge instances, each with 10 Gbps of bandwidth, 32 virtual CPUs and 128 GB of memory, spread across five regions: N. Virginia, N. California, Sydney, Stockholm and Tokyo. The baseline parameters are 4 validators with one worker each, 500 KB batches, 1 KB blocks, 512-byte transactions, and one benchmark client per worker submitting at a fixed rate for 5 minutes. Each point in the graphs is the average of 2 runs, with error bars of one standard deviation. Latency is the time from a client submitting a transaction to the leader that proposed it committing it.

One worker per validator

The first experiment is the head-to-head comparison: a WAN, no faults, one worker collocated with each primary, and committees of 10, 20 and 50 validators (10 and 20 for Baseline-HS). Figure 7 plots it. The horizontal axis is throughput and the vertical axis is latency, and each point is one input rate. The Baseline-HS number looks low until it is set next to the ones the authors cite: 3,500 tx/s for HotStuff on a LAN and around 500 tx/s for LibraBFT. Batched-HS is the instructive row. It is the same consensus code with dissemination taken off the critical path, and the authors put the gain at almost 20x. Its throughput falls as the committee grows. Narwhal-HS goes the other way, which the authors attribute to the worker implementation not using each machine’s network, disk and CPU fully, so that more validators multiplex those resources better.

The two Narwhal systems differ mainly in latency, which is what the paper’s hypothesis predicts: the mempool determines throughput and the consensus on top determines how long a transaction waits. Tusk’s 3 seconds is more than its theoretical 4.5 rounds would suggest. The authors give two reasons. The implementation puts transactions in the blocks of every round, not only a wave’s first round, which adds half a round in expectation. And synchronization is deliberately conservative, so a validator takes a little longer to collect the full causal history before it can order.

Scaling out with workers

For the scale-out experiment every worker and every primary gets its own instance, so a validator with 4 workers is 5 machines. Workers sit in the same data center as their primary, and the 4 validators are spread over the five regions. The runs use 4, 7 and 10 workers per validator, with batches of 1,000 transactions. Figure 8 reports it in two plots.

The upper plot has the axes of Figure 7, with green for Tusk and blue for Narwhal-HS, and solid, dashed and dotted lines for 4, 7 and 10 workers. The bends come in three groups, one per worker count, while the flat parts stay at the same height. In the paper’s words, configurations with more workers saturate later at the same latency, which they take as proof that the primary is far from saturated even with 10 workers feeding it hashes. The lower plot has workers per validator on the horizontal axis and throughput on the vertical, in tx/s on the left and MB/s on the right. The lines are close to straight: the points fall close to the number of workers times the throughput of one worker. The abstract summarizes this as linear growth to 600,000 tx/s with no latency increase, and the introduction as over 500,000 tx/s at under 3.5 seconds. Read off the lower plot, the 10-worker points sit between 500,000 and 600,000 tx/s, and a single worker in this 4-validator setup delivers about 50,000.

Crash faults

The fault experiment repeats the comparison of all four systems with 10 validators and 1 or 3 of them crashed, 3 being the most that 10 can tolerate. Figure 9 has the same axes and colors as Figure 7, with the latency axis now reaching 40 seconds. Solid lines are runs with one fault and dashed lines runs with three. The red and orange curves stay against the left edge and climb; the green and blue ones still extend to the right.

With three faults, Baseline-HS throughput drops 5x from an already low 800 tx/s and its latency rises 40x. Batched-HS drops 30x, from 70,000 to 2,500 tx/s, with latency up 10x. The paper puts the latency of both at 35 to 40 seconds. Table 2 gives the latency of the two Narwhal systems.

Tusk and Narwhal-HS keep a good level of throughput, and the authors attribute most of the loss to the missing capacity of the crashed machines. The text gives no number; in Figure 9 the one-fault curves run to about 120,000 tx/s and the three-fault curves stop short of 80,000. Tusk’s latency grows because the coin elects an absent leader more often. Narwhal-HS is slower than Tusk, as the paper’s theoretical comparison predicts for a leader-based protocol, and still far ahead of the other HotStuff variants. The authors conjecture two causes: consensus over Narwhal has very little data to carry, and the first successful commit picks up a large fraction of recent transactions thanks to 2/3-Causality.

All of these are crash faults. The paper notes that evaluating BFT protocols in the presence of Byzantine faults is still an open research question.

How the paper positions itself

Against Mir-BFT (Stathakopoulou et al., 2019), which reaches about 80,000 tx/s under 2 seconds for 20 WAN validators and falls to zero throughput for up to 50 seconds after faults, the authors report Tusk with one worker slightly higher at double the latency, and about 9x higher with multiple workers. Among asynchronous protocols, they cite Dumbo2 at 5,000 tx/s within 5 seconds on 8 WAN nodes and claim a 20x speedup.

Relative to DAG-Rider, Tusk replaces classic reliable broadcast with the pull-based scheme, shortens the commit rule, and removes weak links. DAG-Rider uses weak links to reference older blocks for block-level fairness, which forces storing every block ever received. Tusk instead re-injects transactions from collected blocks, settling for transaction-level fairness. The authors estimate DAG-Rider itself would take under 200 lines on top of Narwhal. They contrast Narwhal with Hashgraph, which lacks a layered threshold-clock structure and relies on local coins, and with Blockmania (Danezis and Hrycyszyn, 2018), neither of which separates availability from consensus.

The paper lists its own limitations. Slow validators are indistinguishable from faulty ones, so distant but correct validators may see their transactions committed late. Clients must resubmit transactions a faulty validator drops; sending to f+1f+1f+1 validators would divide bandwidth by O(n)O(n)O(n), so the authors suggest a small fixed number. Spreading data across workers burdens the execution engine and light clients, which must locate transactions across machines. And the throughput only matters if an execution engine can keep up, which the authors defer to future work. Their concluding claim is that the main cost of large-scale blockchain protocols is reliable transaction dissemination rather than consensus, and that dissemination without global sequencing is embarrassingly parallel.

My take

The most convincing result in this paper belongs to Batched-HS. The same HotStuff goes from 1,800 to 70,000 tx/s once the leader proposes hashes, and falls back to 2,500 when three of ten validators crash. Everything Narwhal adds, the certificates and the rounds, exists to close that second gap, and the fault experiment shows that it does.

The idea transfers to a permissioned ordering service only in part. In the BFT orderer we built for Fabric, a follower verifies every transaction in the leader’s proposal before it votes. Narwhal’s validity conditions cover the creator’s signature, the round and the certificates. They say nothing about what is inside a batch. So the mempool certifies the availability of data nobody has validated, and filtering moves to an execution layer the paper leaves out.

The headline numbers also matter less for that setting than they look. A consortium that needs a few thousand transactions per second gains nothing from 130,000, and it pays in latency: about 1 second for Baseline-HS at low load, under 2 for Narwhal-HS, about 3 for Tusk. What it would be buying is the fault behavior.

That is where the evaluation is thinnest. It tests crashes and nothing else, and the authors say so. The adversary this design should worry about stays up, signs for batches, and is slow or selective when asked to serve them. The pull path is argued to need O(1)O(1)O(1) requests on average and O(n)O(n)O(n) under attack, and it is never measured. Each point averages two five-minute runs. The scale-out result uses 4 validators with workers in the primary’s data center, and the comparisons with Mir-BFT and Dumbo2 use numbers reported in other papers.

Operationally, “add workers” is a bigger ask than it sounds. Ten workers and a primary are eleven machines, and every organization in the committee has to run that cluster. The paper treats a validator as either correct or faulty. It does not say what happens when one worker loses its disk after the primary has signed for the batches on it. Garbage collection bounds memory, not storage: old rounds go to a store the paper describes as a CDN or S3 and does not evaluate. At 600,000 tx/s of 512-byte transactions each validator’s store grows by about 300 MB every second.

Before adopting it I would want the fault experiments rerun with a validator that withholds batches. I would want to know how a validator that was offline past the garbage collection round catches up. And I would ask what a membership change costs, because the paper works with a fixed committee and Tusk’s coin depends on a threshold key setup.
