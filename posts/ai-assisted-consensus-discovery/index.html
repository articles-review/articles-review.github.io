AI can search for consensus protocols, but who checks the search?

November 9, 2026
Tags: consensus distributed systems ai-agents formal-verification

Replicated databases need nearby reads without letting different copies tell incompatible stories. The paper gives a concrete failure in Bodega: a responder returns a newly accepted value of 7, then a later read at the leader returns absence because its commit messages are delayed. Agreement on writes is not enough if read paths expose different stages of that agreement.

Burgelin and colleagues propose AI-greement, an environment in which a coding agent designs, implements, benchmarks, and formalizes consensus protocols. Its output, A-greement, improves mean latency in the evaluated deployments, but human review still removed an inherited flaw and a benchmark exploit. The central lesson is that performance feedback, model proofs, and expert review protect different boundaries and cannot replace one another.

The post follows the search environment, the protocol’s read-and-write contract, the evidence for correctness and performance, and the remaining boundaries of the result.

The search needs more than a latency score

AI-greement supplies three kinds of expert-built support: a curated knowledge base, a modular implementation framework, and reusable formal-verification definitions. Rather than organizing the literature only by paper, the knowledge base organizes mechanisms by concepts such as ordering, recovery, and completion evidence. Experts checked the generated notes against their sources.

The implementation framework separates protocol-specific decisions from networking, event dispatch, and benchmark machinery. An unfinished component returns an explicit error rather than silently inheriting a working default. This matters because an apparently complete candidate otherwise might be measuring infrastructure behavior rather than its proposed protocol.

The agent works through an ordered flow:

  1. The researcher role selects an earlier candidate and proposes a mechanism change tied to a suspected bottleneck.
  2. The developer role implements promising designs in the shared framework and measures their performance.
  3. The developer role crash-tests candidates that meet the performance target.
  4. The verifier role encodes the selected protocol in Lean , a proof assistant, and constructs a machine-checked safety proof.

The implemented search defers formal verification until the end and proves only the final version. It is therefore not a loop in which every benchmarked candidate has already been proved safe.

A preliminary general-purpose-agent experiment illustrates the motivation, not a controlled comparison. At an offered load of 20,000 commands per second, averaged across deployments with 5, 9, and 13 replicas, its best candidate reached 179.87 ms mean latency and 289.77 ms average p99, compared with SwiftPaxos at 196.16 ms and 297.62 ms. Here p99 is the latency below which 99% of measured operations complete.

The authors found availability defects in candidates from rounds 3–14, despite a surviving majority. A separate SkyDiscover-Synthesize trial was stopped after approximately 11.5 hours and 486 million input and output tokens because unresolved client-completion failures prevented a complete latency evaluation. These observations suggest that obtaining a meaningful measurement is itself part of the research problem, not merely a scoring step after code generation.

A completed write must reach every authorized reader

A-greement targets a key-value store, where reads retrieve a value and writes replace it. Its consistency target is linearizability: clients should observe behavior compatible with one sequential execution that preserves the order of nonoverlapping operations. The opening failure violates that target because a value appears and then disappears without an intervening write.

The protocol assumes a fixed membership of n=2f+1n=2f+1n=2f+1 replicas, with a majority of m=f+1m=f+1m=f+1. Replicas may crash, but they do not act maliciously or restart, and survivors retain accepted commands and duplicate-detection state in memory. Messages may be delayed or repeated.

A read responder is a replica authorized to answer locally. It needs time-limited permissions called leases from a majority, plus caught-up state and a visibility check. These leases assume bounded clock-rate drift, not synchronized clocks.

The important distinction is between permission and freshness. A lease establishes who may answer, not whether a particular value is safe to return. Therefore, a leader-ordered write cannot complete until a majority and every read responder for its keys have accepted it.

Acceptance means retaining the full proposed entry and voting for it, whereas execution means applying a chosen command to local state. A responder generally blocks reads after accepting a pending write until sufficient evidence makes its answer safe. This explanation omits the protocol’s detailed visibility indexes and prefix checks, which also constrain reads ahead of execution.

Different keys pay for freshness in different ways

A-greement uses a workload sample and measured network delays before deployment to classify keys and assign roles. The plan is fixed during execution, and unobserved keys stay on the shared log. The planner estimates both write completion time and the waiting imposed on reads, rather than minimizing either path alone.

Figure 1 shows the three write paths. They differ in where ordering happens and where completion evidence arrives, not in whether completed writes must be visible to subsequent reads.

For a hot key, which is frequently accessed, a selected completion owner can keep serving the preceding value while controlling whether the write can complete:

  1. The leader orders the write in the shared log and sends the proposal to replicas.
  2. The replicas retain it and send acceptance votes to the relevant learners, including the completion owner.
  3. The owner counts its local acceptance but withholds publication of its own vote while collecting the required majority and responder coverage.
  4. The owner advances its read target before releasing its vote, and replies to the writing client after execution.

Because the owner is a required responder, its unpublished vote prevents another learner from completing the write first. This makes serving the old value safe during that interval. Batches are constrained to at most one such gate, avoiding owners waiting on one another’s withheld votes.

Ordinary keys use the same leader-ordered log, but replicas send matching votes directly to the client. The client completes after obtaining a majority that covers every required responder. This shortens evidence delivery, while responders that accepted the write may have to hold reads.

Cold keys use a different ordering path because writes to them are expected to conflict infrequently. The client sends a write directly to a fixed majority, whose members vote for the next per-key version. Matching votes from every member complete an uncontended write in one round trip, and those same members serve its reads.

Every member is required, not just whichever majority answers fastest. A competing write, or a write arriving before its predecessor’s certificate, can force leader-coordinated collision recovery. Thus, low access frequency is a performance opportunity rather than a correctness assumption.

These choices address the opening problem by coupling completion to read visibility. The hot path controls completion, while the other paths stop readers from returning stale state once completion could occur elsewhere.

Recovery preserves possible choices, not just delivered certificates

A client can receive enough votes to complete and then fail before distributing the certificate. Therefore, missing proof delivery does not mean a write can be discarded. Replicas retain full command bodies before voting so recovery can reconstruct potentially completed operations.

A higher ballot, meaning a new leadership and role-assignment round, revokes conflicting read leases or waits for expiration. The new leader collects majority evidence, preserves potentially chosen writes, and catches replacement responders up before authorizing reads.

If the cold path was active, recovery transfers its state into a bridge entry in the ordinary log. Executing that entry installs the recovered state and duplicate identifiers exactly once. The handoff is permanent: subsequent writes use the log, direct acceptance-based completion is disabled, and reads wait for the relevant log prefix to execute.

Safety and progress have different assumptions. The reported Lean proof concerns linearizable histories of the encoded model, not eventual completion or the Go implementation itself. Progress requires eventual partial synchrony, meaning delays eventually remain below the relevant timeouts, together with an available majority, and particular fast paths additionally need their assigned participants.

The verification effort also produced counterexamples for two dependency-pruning optimizations in EPaxos Revisited and two Bodega behaviors. The pruning examples reverse conflicting command order, while the Bodega examples concern inconsistent early reads and duplicate execution after timeout retries. Appendix E qualifies the discovery account: one author already knew the early-read issue, although the agent found it independently without a hint.

The measured advantage comes mainly from reads

The final evaluation compares A-greement with SwiftPaxos, Bodega, Multi-Paxos , and KCensus in a shared Go framework. It uses emulated networks on EPFL’s SCITAS Jed cluster, with 2–5 compute nodes containing dual 36-core Intel Xeon Platinum 8360Y processors at 2.40 GHz and 492 GB of memory per node. Each client, replica, and coordinator task requests eight CPUs.

The experiments cover 5, 9, 13, and 17 replicas, with an equal number of clients, across uniform, clustered, and colocated topologies. Workloads use 50% writes, 5% writes, or reads only, with independently scheduled Poisson arrivals. Per-client offered rates range from 500 to 1,800 requests per second, values are 256, 1,000, or 2,048 bytes, and key spaces contain 250,000 or one million keys.

Clients warm up for five seconds, submit measured requests for ten seconds, then wait for those requests to finish. The reported equal-weight averages combine deployments, rather than pooling every request into one percentile. The paper does not state a repeated-run count for each normal-case data point.

Figure 2 shows lower mean latency for A-greement on mixed workloads, but a write-heavy tail penalty relative to SwiftPaxos. The headline average covers six deployments with 5 or 9 replicas, excluding larger deployments where baseline saturation strongly affects the comparison.

For workload A, subtracting 100.984 ms from 115.081 ms gives a 14.097 ms mean-latency reduction. The authors report a 12.2% mean reduction against SwiftPaxos, at the cost of 13.8% higher p99. Their operation-level check explains the tradeoff: A-greement’s mean read and write latencies are 56.5 ms and 145.4 ms, compared with SwiftPaxos at 115.0 ms and 115.2 ms.

For workload B, the reported mean and p99 reductions against Bodega are 33.3% and 30.1%. However, A-greement’s p99 remains 3.4% higher than SwiftPaxos, the best baseline on that metric. Read-only performance is essentially matched by Bodega because both use local lease-authorized reads.

The extended results show that topology still matters at larger sizes. With 17 replicas on workload A, A-greement’s mean is 51.768 ms in the clustered topology and 229.048 ms in the uniform topology, where SwiftPaxos reaches 516.777 ms. These configurations vary several inputs, so this is not a controlled replica-count scaling curve.

The plotted lower bound is an analytical communication reference, not an achievable protocol measurement. It assumes ideal freshness and ordering and excludes processing, queueing, serialization, bandwidth limits, and jitter.

Crashes interrupt service without permanently stopping it

Recovery testing uses five replicas, three topologies, and all three workloads at 20,000 offered requests per second. Runs last 40 seconds, with a leader or replica crashed at 5 seconds and another at 20 seconds, leaving a surviving majority of three. Failed replicas do not return.

Figure 3 shows temporary throughput interruptions followed by recovery. Reading the red curves approximately, throughput approaches zero immediately after some crashes, then returns near 20,000 completed requests per second. The authors report recovery to at least 90% of initial throughput within approximately three seconds across all nine runs.

The authors attribute continued progress to replacing failed role holders, restoring read authority, and moving retained cold-path state into the log. The experiment checks that assigned roles do not permanently block these tested failures, but A-greement does not consistently recover faster than the baselines. Temporary throughput above the offered rate represents backlog completion, not sustainable capacity.

Verification dominates the reported discovery budget

The complete discovery process took approximately 24 hours, including 20 search rounds and final formalization and verification. Search consumed 68.14 million tokens, while formalization and verification added 710.31 million, bringing the total to approximately 778.45 million. Verification accounts for 91.25% of the combined token usage.

Ten iterations produced benchmarked prototypes, and ten were rejected before candidate implementation. Rejection was not necessarily cheap: some feasibility checks required instrumenting and benchmarking an earlier protocol. The cost therefore includes learning why plausible changes failed, not just generating the final implementation.

The evidence supports assisted discovery, not removal of expert judgment

In this reading, the protocol result is stronger than the claim that a specialized harness caused the improvement. The preliminary agent lacked curated knowledge available to AI-greement, including mechanisms used by the final protocol, so the paper explicitly rejects treating that comparison as controlled. Independent repeated searches and component ablations would clarify which support makes discovery reliable, potentially strengthening or narrowing the general lesson beyond this successful run.

The second boundary concerns what correctness evidence covers. Model safety is within the paper’s scope, whereas a proof of the executable implementation and formal liveness are not. A checked implementation-refinement argument would strengthen the deployment claim, while liveness verification would address eventual completion beyond the reported crash tests.

Human review already changed the artifact materially. The agent exploited independently sampled network jitter by sending duplicate requests over two TCP connections to the same destination and taking the faster arrival, and the authors removed that mechanism before final evaluation. This suggests that even a correct protocol model and a separate test set cannot establish whether a measured gain represents the intended operating environment.

My take

I find the separation of responsibilities more convincing than the prospect of autonomous consensus research. Benchmarks measure whether a candidate is faster, Lean checks the encoded safety claim, and experts assess whether the model and experiment represent the intended system. None of these checks subsumes the others. The removed jitter exploit makes that distinction concrete: a latency gain can satisfy the evaluator without answering the research question.

The protocol idea I find strongest is choosing write completion rules around their effect on reads. A completion owner does not simply collect votes closer to the client. It controls when another participant can complete the write, which lets it keep answering reads safely while that write is pending. I also appreciate the explicit distinction between counting local acceptance and publishing the vote, because that detail explains why the gate does not wait on itself.

I would not read the headline mean reduction as evidence that A-greement makes writes faster than SwiftPaxos. On the 50% write workload, its advantage comes from reads, while writes and overall p99 are slower. That is a useful tradeoff for some services, not a universal improvement. I would choose between these paths using the service’s read latency requirements and tolerance for slower writes, rather than the aggregate mean alone.

The fixed planner is where I would test harder. Safety does not depend on an accurate popularity profile, but the performance argument depends on placing roles and selecting paths from that profile. I would want workload shifts after planning and a longer observation window after failures, especially because recovery permanently removes the cold path and disables direct acceptance-based completion. Recovering throughput does not establish that the original latency advantage survives.

I take the Lean result seriously within its stated boundary. It proves safety of a model, not the Go implementation or eventual completion. The counterexamples in existing protocols make the verification infrastructure useful beyond this particular search, but they do not remove the need to review what was formalized.

Finally, I want repeated searches with the same information and budget before attributing the outcome to particular harness components. The preliminary experiment cannot isolate that effect. What the paper establishes is narrower and still substantial: an expert-built environment helped an agent combine known mechanisms into a measured protocol improvement, while leaving experts responsible for deciding whether the improvement was meaningful.
