Two-step optimism, three-step totality: paired witnesses for reliable broadcast

October 14, 2026
Tags: reliable broadcast byzantine-fault-tolerance distributed computing lower bounds dag consensus

Reliable broadcast lets one designated party, the broadcaster, hand a value to nnn parties so that the honest ones end up agreeing on it, even when up to fff of them are Byzantine, meaning they can behave arbitrarily. DAG-based consensus protocols run one broadcast instance per block per round. Every step a broadcast takes therefore shows up in commit latency, and every step by which one honest party can lag behind another shows up in how long timeouts must be.

The paper by Nikita Polianskii (IOTA Foundation) asks what it costs to make signature-free reliable broadcast fast in the common case. It proves that, for 3f+1≤n<4f3f+1 \le n < 4f3f+1≤n<4f, any deterministic protocol that delivers in two steps whenever at most n−3fn-3fn−3f parties are faulty must let honest deliveries spread over at least three steps. It then gives two protocols. Confirm/ack keeps the largest possible optimistic fault budget across the whole range and reaches latencies (2, 3, 4). Paired witnesses reach (2, 3, 3), which matches the lower bound over a stated parameter range. The paper’s summary is that, in the matching range, maximal optimism costs exactly one totality step relative to Bracha’s classic protocol and no step of good-case latency.

Reliable broadcast and its three clocks

The paper’s model has n≥3f+1n \ge 3f+1n≥3f+1 parties, one of them the broadcaster. An adaptive adversary can corrupt up to fff parties during the execution; a party is honest if it is never corrupted. Parties communicate over pairwise authenticated channels: a receiver knows who sent a message, but cannot prove to a third party what it received. There are no signatures, no trusted setup, and no correlated state carried from earlier instances. Messages between honest parties are eventually delivered, in any order.

A protocol is a reliable broadcast protocol if it satisfies three properties. Validity: with an honest broadcaster, every honest party eventually delivers the broadcaster’s value and nothing else. Agreement: no two honest parties deliver different values. Totality: if any honest party delivers, every honest party eventually delivers. Totality is what makes a locally delivered value safe to act on inside a larger protocol, because a party knows the others will catch up.

Latency is measured in steps, where one step is δ\deltaδ, the actual delay bound on messages between honest parties in a given execution. The protocol is not told δ\deltaδ; a latency bound must hold for every δ>0\delta > 0δ>0. The paper writes latency as a triple (do,ds,dt)(d_o, d_s, d_t)(do​,ds​,dt​):

  • dsd_sds​, the good-case latency, is the worst time from an honest broadcaster’s invocation until all honest parties have delivered, over executions with up to fff Byzantine parties.
  • dod_odo​, the optimistic good-case latency with budget bbb, is the same quantity restricted to executions with at most bbb Byzantine parties, for an optimistic fault budget b≤fb \le fb≤f.
  • dtd_tdt​, the totality delay, is the supremum over all executions, with any broadcaster, of the time from the first honest delivery to the last. This is the elapsed convention of Yu et al. (2026).

The paper also splits totality into dtHd_t^HdtH​ and dtBd_t^BdtB​, the totality delay restricted to honest and Byzantine broadcasters, with dt=max⁡{dtH,dtB}d_t = \max\{d_t^H, d_t^B\}dt​=max{dtH​,dtB​}. Since every honest-broadcaster delivery happens between invocation and the good-case deadline, dtH≤dsd_t^H \le d_sdtH​≤ds​ always holds.

Bracha’s 1987 protocol is the reference point. The broadcaster proposes, parties echo the proposal, a party sends ready after enough echoes or after f+1f+1f+1 readies, and delivers after 2f+12f+12f+1 readies. It is (3, 3, 2): three steps in the good case, and once an honest party delivers, its quorum contains f+1f+1f+1 honest readies that reach everyone within a step, trigger relayed readies, and complete delivery within a second step. The paper points out that an early first delivery does not shrink this to one step under nonuniform delays: the honest readies in the early quorum still take a full step to reach others, and the readies they trigger take a second.

The price Opt-RBC pays for two-step delivery

Abraham, Ren, and Xiang (2021) showed that two-step delivery under all fff faults requires n≥4fn \ge 4fn≥4f without signatures. Shrestha et al. (2025) relaxed the goal: deliver in two steps only when at most bbb parties are actually Byzantine, and fall back to slower delivery otherwise. For 3f+1≤n<4f3f+1 \le n < 4f3f+1≤n<4f they showed the largest feasible budget is

b∗=⌊n/2⌋−f. b^* = \lfloor n/2 \rfloor - f. b∗=⌊n/2⌋−f.

Their protocol, Opt-RBC, attains this budget. The paper uses the corrected arXiv v4 of May 2026, which adds an acknowledgment layer to fix a termination issue in the earlier CCS 2025 version. In the paper’s convention Opt-RBC is (2, 4, 4): good-case latency rises from Bracha’s three steps to four, and totality delay from two to four. Shrestha et al. report a bad-case latency of five, but that is measured from invocation; four is the elapsed first-to-last figure. A superseded v2 of their paper had claimed two-step optimism together with a three-step good case.

The paper’s question is whether this price is necessary: can maximal optimism coexist with Bracha’s three-step good case, and with his two-step totality? The stakes come from consensus. In protocols that disseminate blocks by reliable broadcast, good-case latency controls how quickly honest proposals become available, and totality controls how quickly the other honest parties catch up once one delivers. When the same delivered evidence drives round advancement, totality also bounds how far apart parties enter rounds, which sizes recovery timers.

A lower bound: early fast delivery imposes a deadline

Theorem 1.1 states: let 3f+1≤n<4f3f+1 \le n < 4f3f+1≤n<4f and n−3f≤b≤⌊n/2⌋−fn - 3f \le b \le \lfloor n/2 \rfloor - fn−3f≤b≤⌊n/2⌋−f. In the paper’s deterministic model, every reliable broadcast protocol with two-step honest-broadcaster delivery under at most bbb faults has totality delay at least three. This already holds over executions with an honest broadcaster and at most fff faulty non-broadcasters. For n≤4f−2n \le 4f-2n≤4f−2, the bound also holds when only non-broadcasters are required to deliver and only their deliveries count toward the totality clock.

The theorem quantifies over very general protocols: arbitrary messages, recipient-specific packets, timers, repeated sends, continued communication after delivery, and parties that read real time. It does not assume threshold rules, bounded message complexity, or a three-step good case. It does not cover randomized protocols, or protocols that are given the actual delay bound. Because the proof uses only static fault sets, it also applies to protocols that tolerate adaptive corruption.

In plain words: if a protocol promises two-step delivery even when as few as n−3fn-3fn−3f parties are faulty, then some honest-broadcaster execution has one honest party delivering very early while another has to wait almost three steps. At n=3f+1n = 3f+1n=3f+1, n−3f=1n - 3f = 1n−3f=1, so even a budget of one fault already costs the extra totality step. Since dtH≤dsd_t^H \le d_sdtH​≤ds​, a protocol with ds=3d_s = 3ds​=3 cannot do worse than three on honest-broadcaster spread, so the bound is tight for such protocols; it says nothing about dtBd_t^BdtB​, which could still be two.

The partition behind the proof

The proof splits the parties into five groups. With s=n−3f−1s = n - 3f - 1s=n−3f−1, which lies between 0 and f−2f-2f−2 in this range, there is a source group S\mathcal{S}S of size f−s−1f - s - 1f−s−1 containing the broadcaster, two endpoint groups EA,EBE_A, E_BEA​,EB​ of size s+1s+1s+1, and two common groups KA,KBK_A, K_BKA​,KB​ of size fff. An anchor party hhh is chosen inside S\mathcal{S}S as an ordinary recipient when S\mathcal{S}S has at least two members; otherwise hhh is the broadcaster itself, which happens only at n=4f−1n = 4f-1n=4f−1 and is the case that needs the convention that the broadcaster’s own delivery starts the totality clock. Six executions and three time scales

Suppose some constant D<3D < 3D<3 bounds totality in every honest-broadcaster execution. Pick small times ε,η>0\varepsilon, \eta > 0ε,η>0 with

Because the protocol’s code does not depend on the delay bound, its initialization is fixed as these are varied, and η\etaη can be made small enough that 3η3\eta3η precedes every timer set during initialization. The proof then builds six executions, linked as Figure 2 shows.

The fast executions FAF_AFA​ and FBF_BFB​ have the broadcaster input AAA or BBB, all delays equal to ε\varepsilonε, and one endpoint group faulty and silent after its initial packets. That is s+1≤bs+1 \le bs+1≤b faults, so two-step optimism forces the honest anchor hhh to deliver by 2ε2\varepsilon2ε.

The forcing executions XAX_AXA​ and XBX_BXB​ keep the anchor’s view identical to the fast execution through 2ε2\varepsilon2ε while slowing everything else down. In XAX_AXA​, the group EBE_BEB​ is now honest but late: it receives every initial packet only at η\etaη. Other honest messages take η\etaη, except that messages sent to hhh by time ε\varepsilonε still arrive after ε\varepsilonε (the anchor exception), and the faulty KBK_BKB​ replays its fast-execution packets to hhh. So hhh delivers AAA by 2ε2\varepsilon2ε in XAX_AXA​ too. All honest delays are at most η\etaη, so the hypothetical bound DDD forces every member of EAE_AEA​ to deliver AAA by H=2ε+DηH = 2\varepsilon + D\etaH=2ε+Dη. The bridges WAW_AWA​ and WBW_BWB​ have a Byzantine broadcaster. In WAW_AWA​, the faulty members of S\mathcal{S}S send their XAX_AXA​ streams toward the AAA side and their XBX_BXB​ streams toward KBK_BKB​. The faulty EBE_BEB​ copies, under its own identity, the stream that honest EBE_BEB​ sends in WBW_BWB​ toward the common groups, and copies the late group’s stream from XAX_AXA​ toward EAE_AEA​. Nontransferable authentication is what permits this copying. The four executions are constructed jointly by increasing time, and every copied packet copies an honest packet sent at least ε\varepsilonε before its arrival, so the construction is not circular.

Two lemmas carry the proof. Lemma 3.1 (causal cut) says that the complete timed histories of KAK_AKA​ agree in XAX_AXA​ and WAW_AWA​ before 2η2\eta2η, and those of EAE_AEA​ agree before 3η3\eta3η, with the mirror statements on the BBB side. The reason is a sender-by-sender comparison: the late group sends nothing noninitial before η\etaη, so its first such packet reaches KAK_AKA​ no earlier than 2η2\eta2η; in WAW_AWA​ the copied stream is silent before JJJ apart from matching initial packets; S\mathcal{S}S and KBK_BKB​ are copied explicitly; and packets from EAE_AEA​ or within KAK_AKA​ arriving before 2η2\eta2η were sent from strictly earlier, already matching histories. A packet from KAK_AKA​ arriving at EAE_AEA​ before 3η3\eta3η was sent before 2η2\eta2η, inside the interval just established.

Lemma 3.2 (common indistinguishability) says every party in KA∪KBK_A \cup K_BKA​∪KB​ has the same complete history in WAW_AWA​ and WBW_BWB​ for all time. Each member of S\mathcal{S}S sends the same streams to the common groups in both bridges, each endpoint identity is honest in one bridge and an exact Byzantine copy in the other, and common-to-common traffic is identical by induction. The private EAE_AEA​–EBE_BEB​ streams differ, but each such channel has a Byzantine end in each bridge.

The contradiction follows. Since H<3ηH < 3\etaH<3η, the causal cut carries the forced delivery of AAA by EAE_AEA​ into WAW_AWA​, and of BBB by EBE_BEB​ into WBW_BWB​. Both bridges are fair asynchronous executions, with honest delays at most JJJ, so totality and agreement apply even though the hypothesis about DDD does not. Totality forces the common parties to deliver, agreement forces AAA in WAW_AWA​ and BBB in WBW_BWB​, and identical deterministic histories make that impossible.

The author places this argument in the line of four-execution indistinguishability proofs by Abraham, Ren, and Xiang and by Shrestha et al. What is new is preserving an early delivery under nonuniform larger delays, fixing the resulting deadline across the history comparisons, and using only eventual totality in the bridges. The source group generalizes the padding set of size at most one in Shrestha et al.’s proof, which is what lowers the fast executions’ fault count to n−3fn - 3fn−3f. The construction needs n≤3f+bn \le 3f + bn≤3f+b, because the common groups total at most 2f2f2f, S∪EA\mathcal{S} \cup E_AS∪EA​ is faulty in a bridge, and EBE_BEB​ is faulty in a fast execution. The paper stresses that this is a limit of the partition, not a proof that smaller budgets escape the bound. At n=4fn = 4fn=4f the source group would be empty although it must contain the broadcaster.

Paired witnesses: binding a confirm to its author’s echo

The construction fixes f≥2f \ge 2f≥2, n=3f+1+sn = 3f + 1 + sn=3f+1+s, and a budget in the range

1≤b≤min⁡{f,⌊n/2⌋−f,2s+3}. 1 \le b \le \min\{f, \lfloor n/2 \rfloor - f, 2s+3\}. 1≤b≤min{f,⌊n/2⌋−f,2s+3}.

Let N=n−1N = n - 1N=n−1 be the number of non-broadcasters. Only non-broadcasters send echo, vote, confirm, and witness messages; every party, including the broadcaster, can send ready and deliver. Each party sends each message type at most once per instance, across all values, and keeps processing after it delivers. A receiver keeps one slot per message type and author: it is filled by the first such message and never changed. Every quorum is therefore a set of distinct authors, and a Byzantine party can appear in quorums for different values at different receivers, but cannot stand in for another party.

Figure 3 shows the message flow. A label on an arrow is the number of distinct authors that triggers the next message, and the gray text marks the three delivery rules. There are two ways from echo to ready, through vote below and through confirm and witness above, and neither passes through an acknowledgment.

The broadcaster multicasts its proposal. A non-broadcaster echoes the first proposal it receives, votes on MMM echoes for a value, confirms on CCC echoes for a value provided it echoed that value itself (the own-echo guard), and sends a witness when it holds an echo and a confirm for the value from each of the same PPP authors. Every party sends ready on RRR votes, RwR_wRw​ witnesses, or f+1f+1f+1 readies, and delivers on QQQ echoes, CCC confirms, or 2f+12f+12f+1 readies. Why confirms alone cannot trigger ready

After an echo delivery, at least Q−t≥MQ - t \ge MQ−t≥M honest parties have echoed, so within one step everyone votes, and votes drive ready. After a confirm delivery, at least C−t≥PC - t \ge PC−t≥P honest parties have confirmed, and the obvious analogue would let PPP confirms trigger ready. The paper shows this is unsafe. Take t=f−1t = f-1t=f−1 and b≥s+2b \ge s+2b≥s+2. The honest echoes behind a vote for vvv and the honest echoes behind a confirm for w≠vw \ne vw=v fit disjointly among the honest non-broadcasters exactly when b≥s+2b \ge s+2b≥s+2; Byzantine slots complete both quorums; and the honest confirmers of www leave 2f−12f-12f−1 other honest parties free to vote for vvv. So RRR votes for vvv and PPP confirms for www can both exist before any delivery. Opt-RBC and confirm/ack reconcile the two paths with an acknowledgment round, which costs a step.

The witness message instead certifies, at its author, PPP identities whose echo slot and confirm slot both hold vvv. A claim by another party to have seen such a pair does not count; only the receiver’s own slots do. Because echo slots are immutable, a paired author’s echo for vvv can never later turn into an echo for another value at that receiver.

Agreement and validity

Lemma 4.2 proves agreement and validity. With a Byzantine broadcaster, honest confirms share a value because two confirm quorums intersect in an honest echoer (2C−N=f+s>t2C - N = f + s > t2C−N=f+s>t); every honest witness has an honest confirmer among its paired authors, so honest witnesses carry that value too. Two RRR-vote quorums intersect honestly (2R−N=f+s+2>t2R - N = f + s + 2 > t2R−N=f+s+2>t), and Lemma 4.1 rules out a witness origin opposite a vote origin. Tracing relays backward, every honest ready leads to an honest origin, so all honest readies share one value. An echo delivery excludes opposite honest votes by Q+M−N=2f+s−2b+1>tQ + M - N = 2f + s - 2b + 1 > tQ+M−N=2f+s−2b+1>t and opposite confirms by Q+C−N>tQ + C - N > tQ+C−N>t; two echo deliveries intersect by 2Q−N>t2Q - N > t2Q−N>t. A confirm delivery excludes an opposite vote quorum by C+R−N=f+s+1>tC + R - N = f + s + 1 > tC+R−N=f+s+1>t together with the single-party fact. The paper notes that Q+M−N>tQ + M - N > tQ+M−N>t holds uniformly for t≤f−1t \le f-1t≤f−1 exactly when b≤⌊n/2⌋−fb \le \lfloor n/2 \rfloor - fb≤⌊n/2⌋−f, which is where Shrestha et al.’s budget cap appears; the other inequalities need only b≤fb \le fb≤f.

Delivery and catch-up within three steps

Lemma 4.3 gives the latencies. With an honest broadcaster, everyone has the proposal by step one. With at most bbb faults, QQQ honest echoes arrive by step two; with up to fff, CCC honest echoes arrive by step two and CCC honest confirms by step three. That also bounds honest-broadcaster totality by three.

With a Byzantine broadcaster, let the first honest delivery of vvv happen at time TTT. Figure 4 traces the three recovery branches.

An echo delivery leaves Q−t≥MQ - t \ge MQ−t≥M honest echoes already sent; by T+δT + \deltaT+δ everyone votes vvv (no earlier opposite vote is possible), at least N−t≥RN - t \ge RN−t≥R honest votes arrive by T+2δT + 2\deltaT+2δ, and delivery follows by T+3δT + 3\deltaT+3δ. A confirm delivery leaves C−t≥PC - t \ge PC−t≥P honest authors that sent both echo and confirm; their pairs reach everyone by T+δT + \deltaT+δ, giving witness, then ready, then delivery by T+3δT + 3\deltaT+3δ. Witnesses have no own-echo guard, so a party that echoed another value still helps. A ready delivery leaves f+1f+1f+1 honest readies, giving relay and delivery within two steps. These finite message chains also prove eventual totality without any delay bound.

Theorem 1.2 collects the result: under the budget range above, there is a signature-free asynchronous reliable broadcast protocol against adaptive corruption of at most fff parties with optimistic latency two under at most bbb faults, good-case latency three, and totality delay at most three. At most 5(n−1)2+2(n−1)5(n-1)^2 + 2(n-1)5(n−1)2+2(n−1) messages are sent while their authors are uncorrupted, including after delivery, since each non-broadcaster sends at most five multicasts and the broadcaster at most two. Local work is polynomial. For n≥4fn \ge 4fn≥4f, b=fb = fb=f is supported, which recovers the elapsed-totality guarantee of Abraham, Ren, and Xiang’s protocol (their Theorem 3, which the paper reads as (2, 2, 3) at full budget).

Where the paired-witness rules stop

Corollary 1.3 combines the two theorems. For f≥2f \ge 2f≥2 and 3f+1≤n<4f3f+1 \le n < 4f3f+1≤n<4f with b∗≤2s+3b^* \le 2s+3b∗≤2s+3, equivalently n≥⌈(10f−3)/3⌉n \ge \lceil (10f - 3)/3 \rceiln≥⌈(10f−3)/3⌉, the smallest totality delay of a deterministic protocol with maximal two-step optimism is exactly three, and a quadratic-message protocol attains it together with the optimal good-case latency three. At n=3f+1n = 3f+1n=3f+1, totality three is optimal for 1≤b≤min⁡{3,b∗}1 \le b \le \min\{3, b^*\}1≤b≤min{3,b∗}. The first maximal-budget case the construction misses is (n,f,b∗)=(22,7,4)(n, f, b^*) = (22, 7, 4)(n,f,b∗)=(22,7,4); adding one party gives the matching case (23,7,4)(23, 7, 4)(23,7,4). The counterexecution corrupts the broadcaster and f−1f-1f−1 non-broadcasters, splits the honest parties into a group UUU of size f+s+1f + s + 1f+s+1 receiving vvv and a group VVV of size fff receiving www, and uses a subset U0U_0U0​ of UUU of size f−1f-1f−1. The schedule lets f+s+2f + s + 2f+s+2 honest parties reach RRR votes and send ready for www, while witnesses lead the f−1f - 1f−1 parties of U0U_0U0​ to send ready for vvv before the www readies reach them. Raising the budget beyond 2s+32s + 32s+3 therefore requires changing the protocol, not only its proof.

Confirm/ack: the full budget everywhere, at four totality steps

The second construction keeps b=min⁡{f,⌊n/2⌋−f}b = \min\{f, \lfloor n/2 \rfloor - f\}b=min{f,⌊n/2⌋−f} for every n≥3f+1n \ge 3f+1n≥3f+1 and every f≥1f \ge 1f≥1. It retains Opt-RBC’s fast echo delivery and vote–ack–ready recovery, and adds a confirm sent on a large echo quorum, delivery on a confirm quorum, and an ack trigger on f+1f+1f+1 confirms. The paper explains its choices with counterexamples at n=10n = 10n=10, f=3f = 3f=3, b=2b = 2b=2, where the thresholds are 5 for vote, 6 for confirm, ack, and ready, and 7 for echo delivery. If ack were triggered by 2f2f2f echoes, or required the acker to have voted for the value, a schedule with four honest parties getting AAA and three getting BBB leaves one party delivering AAA on six confirms while only four parties can ack AAA, so nobody sends ready. Under the actual rule, all seven honest non-broadcasters ack AAA on the four honest confirms, including those that voted for BBB. If the ack layer were removed and ready sent directly on 2f2f2f votes or f+1f+1f+1 confirms, a schedule makes four honest parties send ready for AAA and three send ready for BBB; Byzantine readies push one party to delivery and the rest are stuck. The paper notes this concerns that shortcut, not a lower bound on message types.

Safety rests on Lemma E.5: once some honest party has QQQ echoes or CCC confirms for vvv, every honest ack in the whole execution, before or after that event, is for vvv. Theorem 5.2 states that the protocol guarantees (2, 3, 4) at budget bbb, and (2, 2, 4) when n≥4fn \ge 4fn≥4f, with O(n2)O(n^2)O(n2) messages. The full-value version sends at most (5(n−1)+2)(n−1)(5(n-1) + 2)(n-1)(5(n−1)+2)(n−1) messages carrying O(n2(B+log⁡n))O(n^2(B + \log n))O(n2(B+logn)) bits, where BBB is the value encoding length. After the first honest delivery with a Byzantine broadcaster, the bounds are four further steps for an echo delivery (echoes, votes, acks, readies), three for a confirm delivery, and two for a ready delivery; with t=1t = 1t=1 the first two shrink to one step each.

Coded paired witnesses for large values

Proposition 5.1 gives a coded realization with an MDS code and a collision-resistant hash. It keeps the (2, 3, 3) guarantees and supported budgets, conditional on commitment binding, which fails only with negligible probability against a computationally bounded adaptive adversary. Communication is O(nL+κn2log⁡n)O(nL + \kappa n^2 \log n)O(nL+κn2logn) bits in every execution, with LLL the value length and κ\kappaκ the hash length; payload traffic has leading bounds 3nL3nL3nL with an honest broadcaster and 6nL6nL6nL in every execution.

The code has dimension k=P=n−2fk = P = n - 2fk=P=n−2f. The broadcaster sends each non-broadcaster its own indexed fragment with a Merkle proof. A recipient echoes its own fragment; a voter must reconstruct and supplies its own fragment if not already sent; a confirm requires reconstruction plus the own-echo guard. Because Merkle membership does not certify a codeword, every reconstruction re-encodes the full vector and checks the committed root. Witness and ready carry only roots. The key argument is availability at every deadline: each possible first delivery leaves at least kkk honest fragments already in flight, so reconstruction never adds a step. A receiver performs at most five full decode and re-encode attempts. The paper notes that Opt-RBC v4 already has a coded variant with the same asymptotic bits, and that its own 3nL/6nL3nL/6nL3nL/6nL counts do not improve on that; the purpose is keeping the paired-witness latencies.

What the analysis shows: one extra totality step and no extra good-case step

The paper has no implementation and no experiments. Its results are theorems, constructions with exact message counts, matching executions, and substitutions into published consensus latency formulas. All latencies are counted in units of the unknown actual delay bound δ\deltaδ.

The headline is the matching range. For f≥2f \ge 2f≥2 and ⌈(10f−3)/3⌉≤n<4f\lceil (10f-3)/3 \rceil \le n < 4f⌈(10f−3)/3⌉≤n<4f, the optimal totality delay at maximal optimism is exactly three, and paired witnesses reach it with good-case latency three and 5(n−1)2+2(n−1)5(n-1)^2 + 2(n-1)5(n−1)2+2(n−1) messages. Against Bracha’s (3, 3, 2) this is one more totality step; against Opt-RBC’s (2, 4, 4) it is one step less on both good-case latency and totality.

My take

The part of this paper I expect to last is the lower bound. It quantifies over arbitrary deterministic protocols, with timers and any message format, and it says that at n=3f+1n = 3f+1n=3f+1 a fast path that tolerates even one fault costs a step of catch-up. That closes a question a protocol designer would otherwise keep trying to engineer around.

The constructions I read with more caution, and the paper supplies the reasons. Opt-RBC needed a corrected version to fix termination. Two natural variants of confirm/ack break totality at ten parties. Proposition B.2 shows that the paired-witness rules, unchanged, lose totality as soon as the budget is one above 2s+32s+32s+3, first at 22 parties. When we built the BFT orderer for Fabric we gave up pipelining to keep the view change simple enough to trust. A broadcast with six message types, whose safety turns on thresholds one apart, is the opposite choice. I would want it model-checked. I would also want the configuration code to refuse a budget outside the proven range, including after a membership change moves nnn.

For the committee sizes I worked with, the interesting result is the plain one. At 7 parties and f=2f = 2f=2 the largest budget is one, and the paper says Bracha with fast echo delivery already reaches (2, 3, 3) there with three message types. Paired witnesses start to matter from f=3f = 3f=3.

The paper compares against Opt-RBC, and there paired witnesses are never worse on any row of Table 4. Against Bracha the same table is mixed. Good-case commit is 4 steps for both. Paired witnesses gain a step only when at most bbb parties are faulty, and they pay for it in every execution: the Simple-IT round timer goes from 5Δ5\Delta5Δ to 6Δ6\Delta6Δ. Whether that trade is worth it depends on how often a deployment runs with no more than bbb faulty parties, and nothing here measures it. The consensus rows are substitutions into other papers’ formulas, as the author says.

Two things would decide adoption for me. The first is state. A party cannot drop an instance at delivery and gets no bound on when it can, and a DAG runs one instance per block. The second is evidence. Channels are authenticated but nothing is transferable, so a delivered value cannot be shown to anyone outside the instance. In Fabric a block carries a quorum of orderer signatures so that a peer can check it without trusting whoever sent it. The paper notes that an interface needing transferable certificates requires a separate argument. For a permissioned ledger that argument is most of the work.
