Fewer consensus messages can still mean slower agreement

November 8, 2026
Tags: consensus performance-modeling blockchains networks

A group of servers agreeing on a shared ledger can waste time in opposite ways. One design makes everyone exchange votes, keeping the network busy; another funnels votes through a leader, which can become the slowest worker. A timer adds another trap: setting it too short can restart work that was about to finish, while setting it too long delays recovery from a crashed leader.

This paper proposes simple equations for understanding those interactions in HotStuff and Istanbul BFT , or IBFT . Its central lesson is that consensus latency depends on where messages accumulate and which operations must wait for one another, so fewer total messages need not produce faster agreement. Beyond blockchains, this suggests a useful way to read distributed-system performance claims: ask which resource must do the work, not just how much work exists overall.

A leader saves communication but concentrates work

Think of collecting approvals for a school event. Everyone could send their approval to everyone else, or a coordinator could collect approvals and announce the result. The coordinator reduces the exchange of messages, but everyone now waits for that person’s inbox.

That analogy captures the communication patterns the paper studies, not the rules that make agreement safe. The performance analysis considers crash faults—validators that stop responding—not malicious validators that send conflicting or strategically delayed messages.

In the paper’s IBFT description, a leader proposes a block, validators broadcast PREPARE votes, and then they broadcast COMMIT votes. In its HotStuff description, votes repeatedly return to the leader, which broadcasts the next message through PREPARE, PRECOMMIT, COMMIT, and DECIDE phases. The modeled HotStuff is this phase-based protocol; the results should not be read as measurements of every implementation or variant bearing its name.

The paper chooses protocols that assume partial synchrony, a timing model in which communication eventually becomes sufficiently well behaved for progress. It describes a quorum as 2f+12f+12f+1 valid votes and gives the correctness condition n≥3f+1n\geq3f+1n≥3f+1, where nnn is the validator population and fff bounds the faulty validators. Those are background protocol conditions, not a new security result from this performance study.

Before adding switches or failures, the authors count processing work in an idealized network with negligible message delay. With no faults and a sufficiently long timer, their approximations are

TH≈4n−f+4vH,TI≈2n+1vI. T_H\approx\frac{4n-f+4}{v_H},\qquad T_I\approx\frac{2n+1}{v_I}. TH​≈vH​4n−f+4​,TI​≈vI​2n+1​.

Here vHv_HvH​ and vIv_IvI​ are the respective rates at which a validator processes messages. The HotStuff count concerns the bottleneck leader; the IBFT count concerns the work each validator processes. These equations assume a continuously busy bottleneck and account for the paper’s treatment of phase overlap, rather than simply counting the votes needed for a quorum.

This gives a concrete explanation for the apparent paradox: IBFT spreads processing across validators even while generating more communication. The authors’ comparison therefore depends on the relative processing rates, not just the protocols’ message patterns.

A physical network changes which inbox matters

The school-event analogy leaves out the delivery system. If approvals pass through a crowded mailroom, distributing the recipients does not necessarily distribute the delivery work.

The paper studies specified variants of two datacenter networks. Its Folded-Clos has separate layers of switches, with validators attached only to the edge layer; its Dragonfly attaches validators to every switch and connects switches in groups. Validators are spread evenly over the available edge switches.

For HotStuff, the model focuses on the leader and the switch serving it. The authors divide progress into barriers: moments when one piece of processing must finish before the next exchange can begin. Between barriers, validator processing and switch forwarding can overlap, so the approximation takes the larger of their processing times rather than adding both in full.

IBFT needs a different simplification because broadcasts originate throughout the network. Its no-full-round-change model is approximately

TI≈max⁡{2n+1vI,MIsI}, T_I\approx\max\left\{\frac{2n+1}{v_I},\frac{M_I}{s_I}\right\}, TI​≈max{vI​2n+1​,sI​MI​​},

where MIM_IMI​ is the message workload at the modeled bottleneck switch and sIs_IsI​ is its processing rate. The topology enters through MIM_IMI​: messages between other validators can still consume a switch’s capacity.

For fixed switch count, the authors’ switch-dominated IBFT model grows quadratically with validator count, while their corresponding HotStuff model grows linearly. But if the network grows while validators per edge switch stay fixed, the IBFT model also has linear scaling. Thus, in this reading, a scaling claim is incomplete unless it says whether the network is being held fixed or expanded.

The authors also derive a crossover condition for the particular regime where HotStuff is validator-limited and IBFT is switch-limited:

sI≈MI4n−f+4 vH. s_I\approx\frac{M_I}{4n-f+4}\,v_H. sI​≈4n−f+4MI​​vH​.

This is an approximate boundary between bottlenecks, not a universal protocol ranking. It applies to the fault-free, no-full-round-change comparison and neglects the small residual network contribution to HotStuff in that regime.

A timeout pays for both impatience and failure

Suppose a working leader is nearly finished when the timer expires. Restarting can add work without fixing a fault. Conversely, if the leader has crashed, a longer timer means waiting longer before trying another leader.

The paper’s timers double after expiry, and a full round change means all validators move away from the same round. Its latency model separates three possibilities: the first leader has crashed, the first leader works but times out, or the first leader works and finishes without a timeout.

For HotStuff on the idealized network, this leads to the approximation

E[TH]≈TH,success+r+(1−r)q1−2rτ0, E[T_H]\approx T_{H,\mathrm{success}} +\frac{r+(1-r)q}{1-2r}\tau_0, E[TH​]≈TH,success​+1−2rr+(1−r)q​τ0​,

where rrr is the crashed fraction, qqq is the probability of a full round change given a working leader, and τ0\tau_0τ0​ is the initial timeout. The derivation treats successive leader failures geometrically and neglects the probability that working leaders time out in consecutive rounds.

With a sufficiently long timeout, qqq becomes negligible, leaving a model-predicted penalty proportional to rτ0/(1−2r)r\tau_0/(1-2r)rτ0​/(1−2r). The IBFT approximation has the same timeout-dependent tail, plus processing costs for its separate round-change messages. This is an expected-delay approximation under crash faults, not a worst-case liveness guarantee against an adversary.

The authors propose setting the initial timeout to the estimated successful processing time plus three standard deviations. For HotStuff on the idealized network, they estimate this distribution from the messages processed by the leader. IBFT needs a more involved approximation because delays can carry across block boundaries and affect the next leader.

The allowance is a tuning heuristic, not a guaranteed deadline. It is most useful when the assumed service-time distribution and the neglected retry scenarios remain reasonable.

Simulations reproduce the reversals, but not every curve

The evidence comes from a Java simulator, not a deployed blockchain benchmark. Message processing times at validators and switches are exponentially distributed by default, and routing randomly chooses among minimal-hop alternatives. The authors set validator processing rate to 1/31/31/3 arbitrarily and express switch rates relative to validator processing; the plotted times are model units, not measured milliseconds.

Each plotted data point comes from one run averaging 500–2000 consensus instances, without confidence intervals. The paper describes simulation as a sanity check for explanatory equations rather than a rigorous numerical accuracy assessment. No production-hardware configuration or end-to-end transaction workload underlies these latency values.

Timer predictions work better than some latency predictions

In the idealized-network HotStuff experiment with 16 validators and two crashes, the mean is approximately 23 near initial timeout 27, rising to approximately 25 at timeout 40, as read from the plot. For IBFT with the same population and crash count, it is approximately 13 near timeout 15 and approximately 15 at timeout 30. The paper reports better agreement in the long-timeout tails than at short timeouts, especially for IBFT.

Figure 3 tests the harder combination of switches, crashes, and retries. With 40 validators and four crashes, the HotStuff curve on Dragonfly(4) is approximately 60 near timeout 65; the IBFT curves are approximately 100 near timeout 85 on Folded-Clos(8,4) and 70 near timeout 60 on Dragonfly(4), read from the plots. The authors report that the IBFT latency model fits these non-clique experiments poorly, although its recommended timeout locations remain useful.

There is also a more repairable approximation error. Taking the larger of two average processing times misses the extra waiting caused when the slower resource changes from instance to instance. The appendix instead approximates the average of the actual maximum, improving the HotStuff fit but making algebraic comparisons harder.

Figure 4 illustrates this tradeoff for HotStuff on Folded-Clos(8,4) with 31 validators and no full round change. Around switch rate 5, simulation is approximately 53, the simple model approximately 48, and the improved model approximately 52, read from the plot.

The evidence supports a bottleneck map, not a deployment calculator

In this reading, the strongest result is the explanation of changing protocol rankings: it connects a visible simulation crossover to a specific pair of competing bottlenecks. The acknowledged IBFT retry mismatch limits a narrower claim—accurate latency prediction when physical-network congestion and round changes interact—and lies inside the paper’s intended scope. Repeated simulations with uncertainty estimates and a better model of timeout probability would establish whether its useful timeout locations persist beyond the tested settings.

A separate boundary is the move from simulated service rates to an operational system. Deployment prediction lies beyond the reported validation: the study does not measure implementation-specific processing costs, arbitrary malicious behavior, or transaction latency through the surrounding ledger stack. Profiling real message processing and testing the predicted crossovers on an implementation could reveal whether the same bottlenecks dominate, or whether omitted costs move the boundary substantially.

My take

I find the bottleneck analysis more valuable than the protocol ranking. Counting total messages hides the distinction between work spread across validators and work serialized at one leader. The paper makes that distinction concrete enough to explain why IBFT can beat HotStuff despite generating more communication. I especially like the comparison between holding switch count fixed and holding validators per edge switch fixed. Those are different scaling experiments, and the model exposes why they produce different conclusions.

The barrier analysis is the strongest part for me. It identifies which processing can overlap and which exchange must wait, rather than adding every cost into one latency estimate. The appendix also makes the price of that simplicity visible: taking the maximum of expected processing times loses some waiting that the expected maximum captures. I would keep the simple expression for reasoning about bottlenecks, but I would not use its crossover boundary as a precise capacity-planning threshold without checking the transition region.

I am less convinced by the timeout prescription than by the explanation of timeout costs. The shared long-timeout penalty follows clearly from the assumed leader selection and exponential backoff. Setting the timer to the estimated mean plus three standard deviations is a useful candidate, but its usefulness depends on the service-time approximation and on neglecting consecutive timeouts by working leaders. Those omissions matter most near the short-timeout region where the model already diverges. I would test service-time distributions with longer tails and correlated delays before treating that allowance as a reliable setting.

The non-clique IBFT retry model is where I would stop trusting numerical predictions. The authors acknowledge its poor fit, and locating a useful timeout does not establish that the model captures the surrounding latency curve. I would also resolve the inconsistent rate denominator in the round-change term between Equation (15) and the later large-timeout comparison before using that expression. Repeated runs with uncertainty estimates would help distinguish stable agreement from agreement in a single simulated run.

I would use this paper to decide what to profile and which parameter sweeps to run: leader processing, switch forwarding, validator placement, and timeout behavior. I would not use it to predict transaction latency or performance under malicious behavior. Its useful scope is narrower: explaining consensus latency under crash faults in the specified datacenter models. Within that scope, it offers a strong corrective to choosing a protocol from message complexity alone.

A Performance Analysis of BFT Consensus for Blockchains. J.D. Chan, Y.C. Tay, Brian R.Z. Yen. arXiv, 2024.
